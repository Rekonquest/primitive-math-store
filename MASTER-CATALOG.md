


Mathematical Primitives: The Atomic Building Blocks of Mathematics

A comprehensive catalog of the fundamental mathematical atoms from which all quantitative understanding is built. Organized by domain and cross-cutting themes including speed, efficiency, compounding, cryptography, data repair, compression, stability, awareness, extraction, copying, arrangement, duality, and meta-generation.









1. Foundational Arithmetic & Number Theory

Unity (1) — The multiplicative identity; the primitive of oneness from which counting emerges.





Zero (0) — The additive identity; the primitive of nullity that enables place-value and the concept of nothing.





Addition — The primitive of combination; merging two quantities into a single measure of totality.





Subtraction — The primitive of removal; measuring the remainder after one quantity is taken from another.





Multiplication — The primitive of scaling; repeated addition that stretches or shrinks a quantity by a factor.





Division — The primitive of partitioning; splitting a quantity into equal parts to find unit measure.





Exponentiation — The primitive of iterated scaling; repeated multiplication that generates rapid growth or decay.





Logarithm — The primitive of inverse scaling; answering 'how many times to multiply' and compressing wide ranges.





Modulus — The primitive of cyclic remainder; extracting the leftover after division, creating periodic structure.





Prime Number — The primitive of atomic divisibility; a number divisible only by itself and one, the indivisible building block of integers.





Factorial — The primitive of combinatorial counting; the product of all positive integers up to n, counting permutations.





Fibonacci Sequence — The primitive of recursive accumulation; each term is the sum of the two preceding, modeling organic growth.





Golden Ratio (φ) — The primitive of proportional harmony; the ratio where the whole to the larger equals the larger to the smaller.





Infinity (—ˆž) — The primitive of unboundedness; the concept of a quantity without limit or end.





Negative Number — The primitive of directional opposition; quantities extending in the opposite sense from positive.





Rational Number — The primitive of ratio exactitude; any number expressible as a fraction of two integers.





Irrational Number — The primitive of non-repeating exactitude; a number that cannot be expressed as a simple fraction.





Complex Number — The primitive of two-dimensional quantity; a number with real and imaginary parts, enabling rotation and phase.





Imaginary Unit (i) — The primitive of perpendicular scaling; the square root of negative one, opening a new dimension of number.





Absolute Value — The primitive of magnitude extraction; stripping the sign to reveal pure distance from zero.





Floor Function — The primitive of downward rounding; the greatest integer less than or equal to a given number.





Ceiling Function — The primitive of upward rounding; the smallest integer greater than or equal to a given number.





Greatest Common Divisor (GCD) — The primitive of shared factors; the largest number that divides two integers without remainder.





Least Common Multiple (LCM) — The primitive of shared periodicity; the smallest number that is a multiple of two given integers.





Congruence — The primitive of equivalence under modulus; two numbers are congruent if they have the same remainder when divided by a modulus.

Variable — The primitive of placeholder generality; a symbol representing an unspecified or changing quantity.





Equation — The primitive of balance assertion; a statement that two expressions have equal value.





Function — The primitive of deterministic mapping; a rule that assigns exactly one output to each input.





Inverse Function — The primitive of operational reversal; a function that undoes the action of another function.





Composition — The primitive of chained mapping; applying one function to the result of another to build complex behavior.





Group — The primitive of reversible symmetry; a set with an associative operation, identity, and inverses.





Ring — The primitive of dual operation harmony; a set with two operations (addition and multiplication) satisfying distributivity.





Field — The primitive of complete arithmetic; a ring where every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse.





Vector Space — The primitive of directional superposition; a collection of vectors that can be added and scaled.





Linear Transformation — The primitive of structured distortion; a mapping between vector spaces that preserves addition and scaling.





Eigenvalue — The primitive of invariant scaling; a scalar by which an eigenvector is stretched under a linear transformation.





Eigenvector — The primitive of directional invariance; a vector that maintains its direction under a linear transformation.





Matrix — The primitive of tabular transformation; a rectangular array encoding linear mappings and systems of equations.





Determinant — The primitive of volume scaling factor; a scalar that measures how a matrix scales space and detects invertibility.





Trace — The primitive of diagonal sum invariance; the sum of diagonal matrix entries, invariant under basis change.





Tensor — The primitive of multi-linear mapping; a generalization of vectors and matrices to higher-dimensional relationships.





Polynomial — The primitive of weighted power summation; an expression of variables raised to powers with coefficients.





Root — The primitive of zero-finding; a value that makes a function or polynomial equal to zero.





Kernel — The primitive of null mapping; the set of inputs that a function maps to zero.





Image — The primitive of reachable outputs; the set of all outputs a function can produce.





Isomorphism — The primitive of structural equivalence; a bijective mapping preserving all algebraic structure.





Homomorphism — The primitive of structure-preserving map; a mapping between algebraic structures that respects operations.





Quotient Structure — The primitive of collapsed equivalence; a structure formed by grouping elements into equivalence classes.





Direct Sum — The primitive of independent combination; combining structures so their components do not interact.





Duality — The primitive of paired perspective; a correspondence between two structures where each fully defines the other.

Plane — The primitive of two-dimensional flatness; an infinite flat surface defined by two intersecting lines.

Sphere — The primitive of surface minimization; the shape with the smallest surface area for a given volume.





Distance Metric — The primitive of spatial measurement; a function defining the 'length' between two points.





Manifold — The primitive of locally flat curvature; a space that appears flat when examined at a sufficiently small scale.





Tangent — The primitive of local linear contact; a line touching a curve at exactly one point, matching its instantaneous direction.





Curvature — The primitive of bending measure; the amount by which a curve or surface deviates from being flat.

Knot — The primitive of entangled loop; a closed curve embedded in three-dimensional space that cannot be untangled.





Simplex — The primitive of triangular generalization; the simplest possible polytope in any dimension (point, line, triangle, tetrahedron...).





Simplicial Complex — The primitive of glued simplex assembly; a space built by joining simplices along their faces.





Boundary — The primitive of edge definition; the set of points separating a region from its complement.





Interior — The primitive of contained space; the set of points inside a shape that are not on its boundary.





Compactness — The primitive of finite coverage; a space where every open cover has a finite subcover, generalizing closed and bounded.





Connectedness — The primitive of unbroken wholeness; a space that cannot be divided into two disjoint non-empty open sets.


4. Analysis & Calculus

Limit — The primitive of approachable value; the value a function approaches as its input approaches some point.





Continuity — The primitive of unbroken mapping; a function with no gaps, jumps, or breaks in its graph.





Derivative — The primitive of instantaneous rate; the measure of how rapidly a function changes at a single point.





Integral — The primitive of accumulated quantity; the total accumulation of a quantity over a region or interval.





Gradient — The primitive of steepest ascent direction; a vector pointing in the direction of greatest increase of a scalar field.





Divergence — The primitive of outward flux density; measuring how much a vector field spreads out from a point.





Curl — The primitive of rotational tendency; measuring the rotation or circulation of a vector field at a point.





Laplacian — The primitive of local average deviation; the divergence of the gradient, measuring how a point differs from its neighbors.





Partial Derivative — The primitive of single-variable sensitivity; the derivative with respect to one variable while holding others constant.





Total Derivative — The primitive of complete change rate; the combined rate of change accounting for all variable dependencies.





Taylor Series — The primitive of local polynomial approximation; expressing a function as an infinite sum of polynomial terms around a point.





Fourier Series — The primitive of periodic decomposition; expressing a periodic function as a sum of sine and cosine waves.





Convolution — The primitive of overlapping blend; an operation combining two functions to produce a third expressing how one modifies the other.





Delta Function (Dirac) — The primitive of point concentration; a generalized function infinitely tall and narrow with total area one.





Measure — The primitive of generalized size; a systematic assignment of a number to a set representing its size or volume.





Lebesgue Integral — The primitive of measure-based accumulation; integration based on partitioning the range rather than the domain.





Uniform Convergence — The primitive of consistent approximation; a sequence of functions converging at the same rate everywhere.





Analytic Function — The primitive of locally power-series representability; a function equal to its Taylor series in a neighborhood.





Harmonic Function — The primitive of Laplacian nullity; a function whose Laplacian is zero everywhere, modeling steady states.





Saddle Point — The primitive of opposing curvature; a point that is a maximum in one direction and a minimum in another.





Inflection Point — The primitive of curvature reversal; a point where a curve changes from concave to convex or vice versa.





Asymptote — The primitive of approach without contact; a line that a curve approaches arbitrarily closely but never meets.





Jacobian — The primitive of local transformation scaling; the determinant describing how a change of variables scales volume.





Hessian Matrix — The primitive of second-order curvature; the matrix of second partial derivatives describing local shape.





Path Integral — The primitive of trajectory summation; integration over all possible paths, central to quantum mechanics.


5. Probability & Statistics

Probability — The primitive of uncertainty quantification; a number between 0 and 1 measuring the likelihood of an event.





Random Variable — The primitive of chance-assigned value; a variable whose possible values are outcomes of a random phenomenon.





Expectation (Mean) — The primitive of average outcome; the long-run average value of a random variable over many trials.





Variance — The primitive of spread measure; the expected squared deviation from the mean, quantifying dispersion.





Standard Deviation — The primitive of typical deviation; the square root of variance, in the same units as the original data.





Covariance — The primitive of joint variability; measuring how two random variables change together.





Correlation — The primitive of linear relatedness; a normalized measure of the strength and direction of linear association.





Normal Distribution — The primitive of bell-shaped randomness; the symmetric distribution emerging from the Central Limit Theorem.





Bayes' Theorem — The primitive of belief updating; reversing conditional probabilities to update beliefs given evidence.





Prior Probability — The primitive of initial belief; the probability of a hypothesis before observing new evidence.





Posterior Probability — The primitive of updated belief; the probability of a hypothesis after incorporating observed evidence.





Likelihood — The primitive of evidence strength; the probability of observed data given a specific hypothesis or parameter.





Markov Chain — The primitive of memoryless transition; a stochastic process where the next state depends only on the current state.





Entropy (Shannon) — The primitive of information uncertainty; the expected amount of surprise or information in a random variable.





Cross-Entropy — The primitive of distribution mismatch cost; measuring the average bits needed when using the wrong distribution.





KL Divergence — The primitive of distribution dissimilarity; measuring how one probability distribution diverges from another.





Maximum Likelihood — The primitive of best-fit parameter finding; choosing parameters that make observed data most probable.





Confidence Interval — The primitive of estimate range; an interval likely to contain the true parameter value with specified probability.





Hypothesis Test — The primitive of evidence-based decision; a procedure for deciding whether data supports a claim.





P-Value — The primitive of evidence against nullity; the probability of observing data at least as extreme if the null hypothesis were true.





Monte Carlo Method — The primitive of random sampling estimation; using repeated random sampling to approximate numerical results.





Bootstrap — The primitive of resampled inference; estimating sampling distributions by resampling with replacement from observed data.





Regression — The primitive of relationship fitting; modeling the dependence of one variable on others.





Central Limit Theorem — The primitive of normality emergence; the tendency of sums of independent variables toward a normal distribution.





Law of Large Numbers — The primitive of empirical convergence; the guarantee that sample averages converge to expected values as sample size grows.


6. Information Theory

Bit — The primitive of binary distinction; the smallest unit of information, distinguishing between two equally likely states.





Byte — The primitive of grouped binary storage; a sequence of eight bits, the standard atomic unit of digital data.





Shannon Entropy — The primitive of average information content; the minimum expected number of bits needed to encode outcomes.





Mutual Information — The primitive of shared knowledge; measuring how much knowing one variable reduces uncertainty about another.





Channel Capacity — The primitive of maximum transmission rate; the highest rate at which information can be reliably sent through a channel.





Noise — The primitive of unwanted disturbance; random variation that corrupts or obscures the intended signal.





Signal — The primitive of intended information carrier; a detectable quantity carrying meaningful data.





Bandwidth — The primitive of frequency range capacity; the width of frequencies available for signal transmission.





Sampling Theorem — The primitive of lossless digitization; the rule that a band-limited signal can be perfectly reconstructed if sampled at twice its maximum frequency.





Quantization — The primitive of continuous-to-discrete mapping; dividing a continuous range into discrete steps for digital representation.





Error Correction Code — The primitive of reliable transmission; adding redundant bits to detect and repair corrupted data.





Parity — The primitive of simple error detection; a single bit indicating whether the count of ones is even or odd.





Hamming Distance — The primitive of code separation; the number of positions at which two codewords differ, measuring distinguishability.





Huffman Coding — The primitive of optimal prefix compression; variable-length codes assigning shorter codes to more frequent symbols.





Arithmetic Coding — The primitive of fractional interval encoding; representing a message as a single fractional number in [0,1).





Run-Length Encoding — The primitive of repetition compression; replacing consecutive identical values with a count-value pair.





Dictionary Coding — The primitive of pattern substitution compression; replacing repeated patterns with references to a dictionary.





Redundancy — The primitive of excess information; the difference between actual code length and the minimum possible (entropy).





Rate-Distortion Theory — The primitive of quality-rate tradeoff; the minimum rate needed to represent a source within a given distortion.





Source Coding — The primitive of efficient representation; removing redundancy to compress data from its original source.





Channel Coding — The primitive of error-resilient representation; adding controlled redundancy to protect against channel errors.





Joint Entropy — The primitive of combined uncertainty; the total uncertainty contained in two random variables together.





Conditional Entropy — The primitive of residual uncertainty; the remaining uncertainty in one variable given knowledge of another.





Data Processing Inequality — The primitive of information non-increase; processing cannot increase the mutual information between variables.





Fano's Inequality — The primitive of estimation error bound; a lower bound on error probability given conditional entropy.

Boolean OR — The primitive of alternative satisfaction; true when at least one input is true.





Boolean NOT — The primitive of logical inversion; reversing truth value, true becomes false and vice versa.





Boolean XOR — The primitive of exclusive choice; true when exactly one input is true, but not both.





Implication — The primitive of conditional consequence; if the premise holds, then the conclusion must hold.





Equivalence — The primitive of mutual entailment; both statements imply each other, having identical truth values.





Turing Machine — The primitive of mechanical computation; an abstract device reading and writing symbols on a tape according to rules.





Algorithm — The primitive of step-by-step procedure; a finite sequence of well-defined instructions to solve a problem.





Recursion — The primitive of self-referential decomposition; a function defined in terms of itself, breaking problems into smaller copies.





Iteration — The primitive of repeated application; executing a block of instructions repeatedly until a condition is met.





Loop Invariant — The primitive of preserved truth; a condition that remains true before and after each iteration of a loop.





Halting Problem — The primitive of undecidable termination; the impossibility of a general algorithm determining whether arbitrary programs halt.





Computational Complexity — The primitive of resource scaling; classifying problems by the resources (time, space) needed as input grows.

Big-Î˜ Notation — The primitive of tight asymptotic bound; describing both upper and lower growth bounds simultaneously.

Reduction — The primitive of problem transformation; converting one problem into another to prove relative difficulty.





Oracle — The primitive of black-box answerer; a hypothetical device that instantly solves a specific subproblem.





Predicate — The primitive of property assertion; a function returning true or false based on whether a property holds.





Quantifier (—ˆ€) — The primitive of universal claim; asserting that a property holds for all elements in a domain.





Quantifier (—ˆƒ) — The primitive of existential claim; asserting that at least one element in a domain satisfies a property.

Diagonalization — The primitive of self-referential construction; a technique showing a set is larger than assumed by constructing an element differing from all listed.





Fixed Point — The primitive of self-mapping stability; a value that remains unchanged when a function is applied to it.


8. Cryptography

One-Way Function — The primitive of easy-hard asymmetry; easy to compute but computationally infeasible to reverse.





Hash Function — The primitive of fixed-size fingerprint; mapping arbitrary data to a fixed-length digest, sensitive to any input change.





Collision Resistance — The primitive of unique fingerprinting; the property that finding two inputs with the same hash output is infeasible.





Symmetric Key — The primitive of shared secrecy; a single key used for both encryption and decryption.





Public Key — The primitive of open secrecy; a key that can be shared publicly for encryption or signature verification.





Private Key — The primitive of guarded secrecy; a key kept secret, used for decryption or creating signatures.





Trapdoor Function — The primitive of hidden reversibility; a function hard to invert without special knowledge (the trapdoor).





Digital Signature — The primitive of verifiable authorship; a cryptographic proof that a message was created by the holder of a private key.





Commitment Scheme — The primitive of hidden binding; committing to a value while keeping it hidden, then later revealing it.





Zero-Knowledge Proof — The primitive of privacy-preserving verification; proving a statement is true without revealing any information beyond its truth.





Merkle Tree — The primitive of cryptographic aggregation; a tree of hashes allowing efficient verification of data integrity.





Elliptic Curve — The primitive of compact hard problem; algebraic curves enabling strong cryptography with smaller key sizes.





Discrete Logarithm — The primitive of exponentiation hardness; the difficulty of finding an exponent given a base and its power in a finite field.





Nonce — The primitive of single-use randomness; a number used once to prevent replay attacks and ensure freshness.





Salting — The primitive of randomized hashing; adding random data to input before hashing to defeat precomputation attacks.





Key Exchange — The primitive of shared secret negotiation; a protocol for two parties to agree on a secret key over a public channel.





Homomorphic Encryption — The primitive of computation on secrets; allowing operations on encrypted data without decrypting it first.





Threshold Cryptography — The primitive of distributed trust; splitting a secret so a minimum number of parties must cooperate.





Side-Channel Resistance — The primitive of leakage immunization; protecting against information leakage through timing, power, or other physical signals.





Post-Quantum Security — The primitive of quantum-hard protection; cryptographic schemes believed secure against quantum computer attacks.
